How the causal loop diagram works

The causal loop diagram is the foundation of this page. Every relationship in the simulator, the sensitivity analysis and the findings comes from the links shown in the diagram. This section explains how to read it, how it becomes a simulation, and how to test it against your own experience.

What a causal loop diagram is

A causal loop diagram is a map of cause and effect. It shows which factors influence each other, and in which direction. It is not a process diagram and not a sequence of tasks. It shows why a situation behaves as it does over time.

Delivery problems are rarely caused by one factor. Faster coding fills the review backlog, a full queue slows development, and time spent fixing what review missed takes hours from reviewing new work. The diagram makes these chains visible, so the effect of a decision can be traced beyond its first step.

The four elements

Variables
The boxes. Each is something that can rise or fall, such as engineering hours or escaped defects. Dashed grey boxes repeat a variable from another sector, so that long lines stay off the diagram.
Links
The arrows. An arrow from A to B means a change in A causes a change in B, with everything else held still.
Polarity
The sign on each arrow. + means B moves in the same direction as A: if A rises, B rises. − means B moves in the opposite direction: if A rises, B falls. The sign describes direction only. A + link is not necessarily good, and a − link is not necessarily bad. More coordination leaves fewer engineering hours; that is a − link, not a criticism of coordination.
Loops
A closed chain of links that returns to where it started. Loops explain why a situation improves or deteriorates by itself once it starts moving.

Two types of loop

Reinforcing loops (R) amplify change. A small improvement leads to a larger one, and a small decline leads to a larger decline. They explain virtuous and vicious circles. This model has nine.

Balancing loops (B) counteract change. They push a situation back towards a limit or a goal, like a thermostat. This model has six.

How to tell them apart. Count the − signs around the loop. An even number, including none, means the loop is reinforcing. An odd number means it is balancing.

The two examples alongside share a link. That is not a drafting accident. Shallow review brings relief within days and costs within months, which is why cutting review time can look successful for a quarter.

Example 1: a reinforcing loop

Trace it from the top. More hours spent on triage and fix leave less time to read changes closely, so review depth falls (−). Shallower review lets more issues through, so escaped defects rise (−). More escaped defects means more fix work weeks later (+).

There are two − signs, an even number, so the loop is reinforcing. Repair work creates the conditions for more fix work. It runs the other way too: protect review time, fewer issues escape, and the fix load falls.

Example 2: a balancing loop

More issues caught before release means more to fix, so fix hours rise (+). Those hours come out of the review budget, so review depth falls (−). Shallower review finds fewer issues (+).

There is one − sign, an odd number, so the loop is balancing. It settles the fix workload at a level, and it does so by finding less. The team looks stable while the issues move into the first loop instead.

Why delays matter

The diagram shows which factors are connected. It does not show how quickly one affects another, and several of the effects here take months. In the model, AI use rises to the set level over 8 weeks, a new hire takes 13 weeks to reach full output, fatigue builds over about 8 weeks and clears over 12, codebase understanding adjusts over 8 weeks, feedback takes 2 weeks to become a retained context, and an issue released into production is found 6 weeks later on average. Delays explain why a staffing change or a cut in review time can look harmless for a quarter. The simulation adds these delays to the diagram.

From diagram to simulation

A diagram alone cannot say how large an effect will be, or which loop will dominate. The simulation turns each variable and link into a calculation, so the loops run together and their combined effect can be measured. Each variable becomes one of three things.

Stocks

Quantities that build up or drain away, and so carry the past into the present. They are the source of delays.

Team size, new hires onboarding, fatigue, codebase understanding, the review backlog, issues in unreleased code, escaped defects, feedback backlog, retained AI learning.

Flows

Rates that fill or empty a stock each week.

Code completed for review, throughput, issues in generated code, self-fixed issues, issues introduced, issues caught before release, false positives, burnout attrition.

Calculated factors

Values worked out afresh each step from the current stocks and the settings you choose.

Demand, coordination overhead, mentoring time, engineering hours, workload per person, code review depth, triage and fix hours, skill coverage, remaining learning headroom.

What the simulation does each step

The model runs in steps of an eighth of a week over 52 weeks, and reports weekly. Each step follows the links in the diagram in this order.

  1. Staffing changes apply. People are added or removed in whole numbers. Removals are taken in proportion to the mix of experienced people and new hires.
  2. Onboarding progresses. New hires move towards full output and take mentoring time from the rest of the team until they get there.
  3. Hours are worked out. Staffed hours less coordination, mentoring, the shortfall of new hires and the time lost to fatigue give engineering hours.
  4. Hours are allocated. Feedback evaluation, the review budget, fix of production issues, then whatever remains for development. Review hours not used return to development.
  5. Work is produced. Generated output depends on the share of work done with AI, the coding gain and the hours available. Hand-written work uses the rest. A full review backlog slows both.
  6. Issues are created. Generated code carries its own issue density, reduced by what the generator fixes itself, by retained learning and by how well the team understands the code. Hand-written work carries its own.
  7. Review and test run. AI review finds a share of the real issues and raises some false positives. People find more, depending on the depth their remaining hours allow. Tests catch a share of what both missed.
  8. Repairs happen. AI fixes some of what was found; the rest is escalated to a person. Every AI fix still costs human verification.
  9. Work is released. Throughput is whichever is lowest: work waiting, review capacity, fix capacity, or the rate the organisation is asking for. Issues not found go out with it.
  10. Value is recorded. Released work adds value, less an allowance for the escaped defects.
  11. Production issues surface. Issues released earlier are discovered after the delay and fixed from the hours held for them.
  12. Feedback and learning update. Outcomes are captured, the team evaluates what its time allows, accepted retained context (prompts, rules and examples) raise retained learning, and stale retained context (prompts, rules and examples) decay.
  13. People update. Workload, fatigue, codebase understanding and departure pressure are recalculated for the next step.

What the diagram and simulation can and cannot tell you

They can show which constraint decides delivery, which loops are likely to drive behaviour, how large an effect has to be before it matters, how long a consequence takes to appear, and which factors the outcome is most sensitive to. They cannot predict your delivery rate, your defect counts or your dates. The strength of each link and the length of each delay are informed estimates until they are checked against your own evidence. Use the results to compare options and focus attention, not as forecasts.

Testing the diagram against your experience

The diagram is most useful when the people who do the work challenge it. When you review it, consider these questions for your own team.

Is the link real?

Have you seen this cause and effect in your team? Can you give an example from the last quarter?

Is the sign right?

Does the second factor really move in the direction shown, or does it depend on circumstances you would want separated?

How long does it take?

Does the effect appear within days, weeks or months? The delays matter more than the strengths.

What is missing?

Is there a response you have seen when delivery gets worse that the diagram does not show?

Which loop dominates?

Which loop best describes what is happening in your area today, and is it running in a good or a bad direction?

Bring your answers to the review session. Anything agreed there can be built into the diagram and the simulation.

What each measure means

Every number on the tiles and every line on the charts is defined here, with its units and the direction that counts as better.

The two units the model uses

A work unit is one unit of finished work. Its size is set by the effort per work unit slider, which is 20 hours by default: one work unit is what one person completes in about half a week of development time, before review. Nothing in the model depends on what that unit is called in your organisation, so read it as a story, a ticket or a change, whichever matches the way you already count.

A value unit is one work unit released, less an allowance for the escaped defects at the time. It is a relative measure for comparing settings. It is not money, and it carries no assumption about what a released change is worth to a customer.

Reading the tile comparisons

The second line of each tile is the difference from the current plan, or from your pinned comparison. It is shown in green when the difference is better for that measure and red when it is worse, which is why a fall in escaped defects shows green while a fall in throughput shows red. A difference of zero is shown in grey. The comparison is always against the same measure, in the same units, at the same point in the year.

Working without exact figures

Nothing here needs a cost model. Three measurements, all available within a quarter, would move most of the weak assumptions to grade A: the hours a change actually takes to review, the number of issues found per change before release, and the time between a change being released and an issue in it being reported. Take them for one period, set them here, and re-run the scenarios.

Limits of the model

It models one team, as an average rather than as individuals, so it cannot show the loss of a particular specialist. It holds no cost or revenue, so it cannot answer whether a smaller team is cheaper. It assumes work is always available to do. It assumes evaluated feedback helps, and does not simulate a harmful item of retained context passing evaluation. Recruitment lead time is not modelled: people you add arrive in the week you choose. The learning parameters are the weakest part: no published source sets a weekly rate of improvement, so they are exploratory settings, not measurements.

What the numbers can and can't be trusted for

This model went through ten rounds of independent, adversarial validation after its first build. That process found and fixed real defects: hours were being allocated to more than one activity at once, a complete release of finished work could strand a third of its defects with no work left to carry them, the "no AI" comparison was quietly still crediting AI-driven repair, and several timing and input-handling edge cases produced silently wrong results. All of that is now fixed and independently confirmed. Throughput, delivery, backlog and workload figures are numerically stable: they do not depend on settings that shouldn't matter, such as how finely the simulation steps through time.

One thing is not fixed. The count of escaped defects, and the value figure that depends on it, still shift by around 15% depending on the simulation's internal time-step — a setting that has no real-world meaning and should not affect the answer at all. Two separate, plausible causes were found and corrected during validation; neither closed the gap, and no further cause has been identified. Treat escaped-defect and value numbers as directional only — useful for comparing "more" against "less" across scenarios — not as a specific figure to quote. Throughput, delivery and backlog numbers do not have this problem and can be read at face value, within the other limits on this page.
